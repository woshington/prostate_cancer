Prostate cancer is one of the most common malignant neoplasms in men, representing a significant challenge for public health~\cite{ref-siegel2024}. Tumor grading plays a central role in defining clinical management and prognosis. The Gleason system, based on the sum of the two most prevalent histological patterns (Gleason score), has evolved into the ISUP grading system (0–5), which provides improved prognostic stratification and is widely used in clinical practice~\cite{ref-epstein2016}.

The traditional histopathological evaluation of whole-slide images (WSIs) relies on manual analysis by pathologists, a process that is subject to inter- and intra-observer variability~\cite{ref-bulten2020}, time-consuming, and limited by the availability of specialists. These constraints have motivated the development of automated deep learning methods.

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have shown outstanding performance in medical image analysis~\cite{ref-campanella2019}, including the automated grading of Gleason patterns and ISUP grades~\cite{ref-nagpal2019,ref-bulten2020}. Among modern architectures, the EfficientNet family~\cite{ref-tan2019} offers an excellent trade-off between accuracy and computational cost, due to its compound scaling strategy and the use of MBConv blocks with squeeze-and-excitation~\cite{ref-hu2018}.


In this work, we investigate the automated classification of ISUP grades by evaluating EfficientNet variants (B0, B3, and B7) on the PANDA dataset~\cite{ref-panda2020}. We employ transfer learning, fine-tuning strategies, advanced training techniques, and ensemble methods to enhance model robustness.
The main contributions of this study include: (1) the implementation of a loss function that combines ordinal and focal components; (2) a comparison of training techniques, including noise reduction, SWA~\cite{ref-izmailov2018}, and focal loss; (3) the development of ensemble models to improve prediction stability; and (4) a systematic analysis of EfficientNet variants for ISUP classification. Performance is primarily evaluated using the quadratic weighted kappa (QWK)~\cite{ref-cohen1968}, an appropriate metric for ordinal classification tasks, along with accuracy.
